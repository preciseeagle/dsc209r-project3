The Shifting Geography and Drugs of the U.S. Overdose Crisis

CDC VSRR Provisional Drug Overdose Death Counts - 12-month-ending totals by state, 2015-2026. Drag the slider through time, switch the drug category, and click a state to trace its trajectory.

Data notes: each value is the number of overdose deaths in the 12 months ending in the selected month. The CDC reports New York City separately, so we added its deaths into New York. Fentanyl and heroin deaths are also part of the opioid count, and a death involving more than one drug counts in every category that applies, so the categories don't add up to the total. Light gray states have no reported value for that month. Rates are deaths per 100,000 residents, using each state's April 1, 2020 Census population for every year.

About this visualization

Design rationale

We wanted to show two things changing at the same time: where overdose deaths are concentrated and which drugs are behind them, over more than ten years. The CDC reports deaths by state, and geography is half of our question, so we built the chart around a choropleth map. States are colored on one sequential red scale, and the color domain stays the same for every month. When you drag the time slider, a darker state really means more deaths, not a palette that has been rescaled.

Counts versus rates was the decision we spent the most time on. Our exploratory plots showed that raw death counts mostly follow state population, so the map kept lighting up the biggest states every month. We added a toggle to switch to deaths per 100,000 residents, which compares states more fairly and changes which ones look worst. We kept raw counts too, since they still answer "how many," and let the reader pick.

We kept the interactions to a few. The time slider shows the long rise and the recent decline. The drug filter isolates the shift from heroin to synthetic opioids. Clicking a state opens a line chart of that state over time, so you get the detail without cluttering the map, and hovering shows the exact value for any state and month.

We tried a couple of other ideas and dropped them. Small multiples (one small map per year) were unreadable with this many months and states, and the slider shows the same change more directly. A stacked area chart of drugs over time got too crowded once we added geography, so we turned the drug breakdown into a filter, which also kept the page down to one map and one trend chart.

Development process

We found our dataset together, each spending about an hour searching before we agreed on the CDC's provisional overdose death counts. We worked over Discord and used separate branches with pull requests to merge changes. Derek built the first version of the interactive (the map, time slider, drug filter, and linked trend chart), the exploratory charts, and the data processing script, and later added the counts vs per-100k toggle. Moose added the per-100k rates and the data notes caption, fixed a bug that left the map blank, set the page to open with a state selected, and added missing axis labels to two exploratory charts. Each of us spent roughly 5 to 7 hours on the project, so about 10 to 14 people-hours in total. Hooking up the D3 code took the longest, since it was new to both of us, and we spent a lot of time researching and reading the D3 documentation. Once we settled on a topic, we stuck with it.
